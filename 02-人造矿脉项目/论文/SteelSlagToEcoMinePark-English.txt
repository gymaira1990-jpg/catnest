    Abstract

    China's iron and steel industry produces over 100 million tonnes of steel slag
annually, with historical cumulative stockpiles exceeding 1.5 billion tonnes and a
comprehensive utilization rate below 30%. The implementation of GB 175-2023
"Common Portland Cement" has further narrowed the direct disposal pathways for steel
slag in cement-based materials. Departing from the conventional engineering mindset of
"rapid disposal," this paper proposes a paradigm shift across spatial and temporal
scales—redefining steel slag landfills as an "artificial vein-ore" ecosystem, in which steel
slag serves as the inorganic skeleton, organic waste as the biochemical engine, and
functional microorganisms together with saprophagous insects act as synergistic drivers.
For the first time, we systematically propose the theory of "Accelerated Artificial
Diagenesis (AAD)." This theory compresses geological-scale natural diagenesis into an
engineering scale of approximately five years through multi-factor synergistic
enhancement, achieving the fundamental transformation of industrial solid waste into
stable "artificial ore" via a three-stage ecological succession of "activation—
stabilization—mineralization." The paper systematically elucidates the multi-waste
synergistic symbiosis mechanism, the microbial mineralization-accelerated carbonation
mechanism, the black soldier fly bioconversion mechanism, the mechanism of active
utilization of steel slag spontaneous pulverization, and key biochemical coupling
processes including organic acid–calcium ion release coupling, bioheat generation–self-
heating maintenance coupling, in-situ CO₂ supply–carbonation coupling, and straw
skeleton–permeability enhancement coupling. Based on the demonstration of technical
feasibility, this paper presents a complete engineering design for a 10,000-tonne-scale
proof-of-concept project, together with a full life-cycle carbon footprint analysis and a
preliminary economic feasibility assessment. The core insight of this study is as follows:
acknowledging the inherent limitations of engineering intervention, we turn to learning
from nature, harnessing time and biological communities—the two most powerful forces
at our disposal—to transform today's "environmental liabilities" into tomorrow's
"resource assets," thereby exploring a novel paradigm for industrial civilization to
transition toward a "geological-scale circular economy" in harmony with the Earth
system.

    1 Introduction


1.1 Scale and Urgency of the Steel Slag Problem

    China is the world's largest crude steel producer, with an annual crude steel output
consistently maintained at the order of 1 billion tonnes, accounting for over 53% of
global production (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024). Based on a generation rate of
100–150 kg of steel slag per tonne of crude steel, China's annual steel slag production
amounts to approximately 100–150 million tonnes. The mineralogical composition of
steel slag is dominated by dicalcium silicate (C₂S), tricalcium silicate (C₃S), calcium
ferrite (C₂F), and the RO phase (MgO-FeO-MnO solid solution). Its chemical
composition (CaO 40–50%, SiO₂ 10–15%, Al₂O₃ 1–5%, FeO/Fe₂O₃ 10–30%, MgO 5–
10%) bears a certain resemblance to that of cement clinker, theoretically offering
potential as a supplementary cementitious material.
    However, steel slag contains 2–10% free calcium oxide (f-CaO) and a certain amount
of free magnesium oxide (f-MgO). The hydration reaction of f-CaO proceeds at an
extremely slow rate, and the formation of Ca(OH)₂ through this hydration is accompanied
by approximately 97% volume expansion, a process that can persist for decades or even
centuries under natural conditions. When steel slag containing insufficiently slaked f-CaO
is used as a building material, the risk of cracking due to delayed expansion poses a
critical unsoundness problem. China's comprehensive utilization rate of steel slag has
long remained below 30%, with historical cumulative stockpiles exceeding 1.5 billion
tonnes (Zhang, 2026).

    Applicability analysis of different types of steel slag. The mineralogical composition
and f-CaO content of steel slag vary significantly depending on the steelmaking process,
which directly affects the transformation cycle and process parameters within the
"Jianggang Plan." Basic oxygen furnace (BOF) slag typically contains 3–8% f-CaO and a
relatively high FeO content (15–25%), with a mineralogical assemblage dominated by
C₂S, C₃S, C₂F, and the RO phase. BOF slag is an ideal feedstock for the "Jianggang
Plan," as its moderate f-CaO content renders transformation within a five-year cycle
highly feasible. Electric arc furnace (EAF) oxidizing slag has an f-CaO content
comparable to that of BOF slag (3–8%) but a higher FeO content (20–35%) and greater
density, requiring consideration of its high density during stockpile construction. EAF
reducing slag can have an f-CaO content as high as 15% or more, along with a substantial
proportion of C₃A (tricalcium aluminate), resulting in extremely high reactivity. For this
type, it is recommended to extend the transformation cycle to 7–8 years, appropriately
reduce the steel slag layer thickness (to 10–15 cm), increase the organic acid contact area,
or adopt a layered alternating arrangement with other types of steel slag. Ladle furnace
slag has an extremely high CaO content (50–60%), a high Al₂O₃ content (20–30%), and
an f-CaO content reaching 10–20%. It is recommended to blend it with BOF slag at a
proportion not exceeding 30%. Stainless steel slag has a high Cr content (2–5%), and
special attention must be paid to the leaching risk of Cr(VI). Its inclusion in the routine
material system of the "Jianggang Plan" is not recommended at present, pending the
maturation of heavy metal stabilization technologies.
    Synergistic effects of mixed steel slags. The blended system of high-calcium ladle
furnace slag (CaO 50–60%) and BOF slag (CaO 40–45%) exhibits significant
complementarity: the ladle furnace slag provides an abundant supply of Ca²⁺, while the
BOF slag supplies Si⁴⁺ and Al³⁺, forming a CaO-SiO₂-Al₂O₃ system that more closely
approximates the chemical composition of cement clinker and favors the formation of
composite minerals such as calcium carboaluminate during the carbonation stage.
Blending EAF reducing slag (high f-CaO) with BOF slag (moderate f-CaO) can
effectively "dilute" the f-CaO concentration in the system, preventing temperature
runaway and structural damage caused by excessively localized vigorous reactions. It is
recommended that the proportion of high-f-CaO slag in the blended system not exceed 30%
(by mass).

    Effect of steel slag cooling method on reactivity. The cooling method employed for
steel slag exerts a decisive influence on its mineralogical composition and reactivity.
Water-quenched steel slag (rapid cooling), owing to its extremely high cooling rate
(>100°C/s), prevents the crystallization of molten silicates, resulting in the formation of a
substantial vitreous phase content (up to 30–50%). The Ca²⁺ and Si⁴⁺ sequestered within
the vitreous phase exist in a high-energy state, conferring strong reactivity, while f-CaO
is predominantly present in microcrystalline or amorphous form and dissolves more
rapidly under organic acid conditions. Air-quenched steel slag (intermediate cooling) has
a vitreous phase content (approximately 15–30%) that lies between that of water-
quenched and naturally cooled slags, with a moderate degree of mineral crystallinity.
Naturally cooled steel slag (slow cooling) exhibits high crystallinity, with f-CaO present
as relatively large crystalline grains (up to tens of micrometers) often encased by minerals
such as C₂S and C₃S, resulting in low reactivity. For the "Jianggang Plan," water-
quenched steel slag is the most suitable primary feedstock, as its high vitreous phase
content and finely dispersed f-CaO facilitate a shorter transformation cycle. Naturally
cooled steel slag requires an appropriate extension of the activation time in Stage I (from
2 years to 3 years), or compensation for its low reactivity by increasing the organic acid
supply.
1.2 Policy Environment: Structural Transition from Acquiescence to
Tightening

    The national standard GB 175-2023 "Common Portland Cement," released in 2023,
imposes stricter limits on the types and proportions of admixtures permitted in cement,
and steel slag has been explicitly excluded from the list of allowable admixtures for
common Portland cement. This regulatory adjustment has effectively closed the
traditional primary pathway for steel slag disposal, rendering the issue of steel slag
valorization even more pressing.

    Concurrently, the "Comprehensive Solid Waste Treatment Action Plan" (Guo Fa
[2025] No. 14, referred to in the industry as the "Ten Measures for Solid Waste"), issued
by the State Council in December 2025, explicitly sets binding targets by 2030, including:
achieving a national annual comprehensive utilization volume of 4.5 billion tonnes for
bulk industrial solid waste, effective control of historical stockpiles, and a substantial
increase in the comprehensive utilization rate of newly generated smelting slag. This
combination of policy "tightening on one front and opening on another" provides an
unprecedented strategic demand and policy window for fundamental innovation in steel
slag valorization technologies.


1.3 Limitations Analysis of Mainstream Technical Pathways

    Current steel slag treatment technologies can be categorized into three major routes:
physical digestion, chemical carbonation, and surface modification.

    The physical digestion route includes the hot-stewing method, the autoclave method,
and the natural weathering method. The hot-stewing method utilizes the residual heat of
steel slag by spraying water to generate steam, which accelerates the hydration of f-CaO
under high temperature and high pressure, reducing the f-CaO content to below 3%.
However, the penetration depth of high-pressure steam is limited, making it difficult to
thoroughly digest f-CaO inside large slag blocks, and both energy consumption and
equipment investment costs are relatively high. The natural weathering method requires
open-air stockpiling of steel slag for 1-3 years; although the cost is low, it occupies
extensive land area and the completeness of digestion is difficult to guarantee.

    The chemical carbonation route utilizes CO₂ to react with f-CaO to form stable
CaCO₃. However, under atmospheric pressure, the carbonation rate of f-CaO is severely
limited by CO₂ mass transfer and the encapsulation effect of the product layer. Although
pressurized carbonation can accelerate the reaction, the equipment investment and
operating costs increase significantly.

    The surface modification route wraps steel slag particles with organic/inorganic
coatings to block moisture contact. Its effect is immediate, but the long-term reliability of
coating durability over decades of building service life has yet to be fully validated.

    The common limitation of the above technical routes lies in the attempt to "conquer"
an intrinsically geological-scale chemical reaction process within an extremely short
engineering timeframe using high energy input, resulting in varying degrees of a "cost-
effectiveness-scale" impossible triangle.


1.4 Paradigm Shift and Core Proposition of This Study

    This paper proposes a fundamental epistemological shift: acknowledging the
limitations of engineering means and transforming the logic from "rapid treatment" to
"controlled transformation." Inspired by the wisdom of the traditional Chinese
"Jianggang" (fermentation vat) long-term fermentation and the natural diagenetic
processes in geology, this paper systematically proposes, for the first time, the theoretical
framework of "Accelerated Artificial Diagenesis" (AAD).

    The core proposition of this theory is: redefining the steel slag landfill site as a
human-designed and controlled giant surface weathering-sedimentation reactor. With a
complete cycle of 5 years, through the synergistic coexistence of multi-source wastes and
the introduction of functional biological communities, the thorough mineralization and
transformation of unstable components in steel slag is systematically completed under
controlled conditions. Over time, the stockpile site successively evolves into a "natural
ecological park" and an "artificial ore park," ultimately achieving a complete
transformation from an "environmental burden" to a "resource reserve."

    2 Theoretical Framework: Scientific Basis and System Design of the Accelerated
Artificial Diagenesis Theory


2.1 Accelerated Artificial Diagenesis Theory

    In geology, "diagenesis" refers to all physical, chemical, and biological changes that
sediments undergo after deposition and before metamorphism, ultimately transforming
loose sediments into consolidated sedimentary rocks. The key processes of diagenesis
include compaction and dewatering, dissolution and reprecipitation, mineral phase
transformation, microbial activity, and the formation of carbonate cements. The timescale
of natural diagenesis is typically on the order of millions to tens of millions of years.

    The Accelerated Artificial Diagenesis (AAD) theory proposed in this paper refers to
the engineering intensification of the driving forces of diagenesis (water, CO₂,
microorganisms) under human intervention, and the engineering design of its spatial
(stockpile) and temporal (5-year cycle) dimensions, thereby compressing the diagenetic
process that requires geological epochs in nature to an engineering-accessible timescale.

    The core strategy of the AAD theory is "multi-factor synergistic intensification":
simultaneously applying three types of driving forces within the stockpile — physical
intensification (leachate recirculation, pulsed spraying, particle size gradation
optimization), chemical intensification (organic acid-promoted f-CaO dissolution, CO₂
injection), and biological intensification (inoculation with functional microbial consortia
and insects) — to generate synergistic amplification effects, with the overall
transformation efficiency far exceeding the simple superposition of individual approaches.

    (b) Active utilization mechanism of steel slag self-pulverization

    The hydration expansion of f-CaO in steel slag is often considered a detrimental
phenomenon, but in the AAD system, it can be actively converted into an advantage.
When moisture penetrates into the interior of steel slag blocks through pores, the
localized expansion stress (up to tens of MPa) generated by the hydration of f-CaO to
form Ca(OH)₂ induces the self-pulverization of steel slag — large slag blocks disintegrate
into fine particles (particle size can decrease from tens of centimeters to a few
millimeters). This process greatly increases the specific surface area of the steel slag (by
2-3 orders of magnitude), significantly enhancing the mass transfer efficiency of
subsequent organic acid dissolution and CO₂ carbonation reactions. In the early stage of
the activation period, through reasonable water spraying scheduling (under stockpile
temperature conditions of 50-60°C), this transformation from "detrimental expansion" to
"beneficial pulverization" can be initiated and accelerated.

    (c) Biological heat generation-self-heating maintenance coupling

    Organic wastes release large amounts of metabolic heat during aerobic degradation.
In a composting system, the internal temperature during the mesophilic-thermophilic
phase can reach 50-75°C. According to the Arrhenius equation, the chemical reaction rate
constant is exponentially related to temperature (k = A·e⁻ᴱᵃ/ᴿᵀ). The self-heating of the
stockpile raises the reaction temperature from ambient temperature (approximately 20°C)
to 55-65°C, which can increase the f-CaO carbonation rate by 5-10 times and the
hydration reaction rate by 10-20 times. This "self-heating-driven" effect enables a
substantial increase in reaction rates within the system without the need for external
energy input.

2.2.3 Microscopic Mechanisms of Microbe-Mineral Interfacial
Interactions

    Microbial transformation of steel slag minerals does not occur throughout the entire
solution but is highly concentrated at the mineral-biofilm interface. Microorganisms
colonize the mineral surface and create localized reaction microenvironments through the
following mechanisms: secreting extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) to form
biofilms, constructing a reaction layer approximately 10-100 μm thick on the mineral
surface; within the biofilm, the concentration of organic acids produced by microbial
metabolism can be 1-3 orders of magnitude higher than that in the bulk solution, forming
a microzone acidic environment that efficiently drives mineral dissolution;
microorganisms actively take up essential elements such as Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ from the
mineral surface through active transport, and induce mineral precipitation on the cell wall
or EPS template through metabolic activity.

    The important implication of this microscopic mechanism for stockpile design is that
microorganisms need full contact with the steel slag mineral surface to achieve maximum
transformation efficiency. Therefore, the bacterial suspension should uniformly penetrate
various parts of the steel slag layer, rather than remaining solely in the pore solution of
the stockpile.


2.3 Biological Synergy: Microbial Mineralization and Insect
Bioconversion

    The core design of the Jianggang (fermentation vat) Plan at the biological community
level is the establishment of a three-tier synergistic transformation system comprising
"microbes—insects—plants."


2.3.1 Functional Microbial Communities

    Microorganisms serve as the "biochemical engine" of the AAD system, with their
core functions encompassing organic matter degradation, organic acid production, and
calcium carbonate mineralization. The functional microbial community designed in this
concept includes the following key groups:

decomposing complex organic matter such as cellulose, proteins, and lipids into small-

cellulose-degrading bacteria (Clostridium spp., Ruminococcus spp.) and acidogenic
bacteria (Lactobacillus spp., Propionibacterium spp.). These bacterial groups have been
widely applied in natural composting and biogas fermentation systems; upon inoculation,
they can initiate the acidification process of the pile body within 2–3 days.

to produce CO₃²⁻ and NH₄⁺ (Eq. 3), promoting CaCO₃ precipitation in an alkaline

to elevate pH and initiate carbonate mineralization. When the phosphorus content of steel

group.

    Microbial community succession and functional redundancy. The microbial
community within the pile body will undergo a predictable ecological succession: Stage 1
(activation period), characterized by abundant organic matter and large pH fluctuations,
during which fast-growing r-strategists (lactic acid bacteria, yeasts, etc.) dominate; Stage
2 (stabilization period), during which organic matter is progressively consumed, pH
stabilizes at 8–10, and slow-growing K-strategists (mineralizing bacteria such as
Sporosarcina pasteurii) establish dominance; Stage 3 (mineralization period),
characterized by nutrient depletion, during which oligotrophic bacteria and endospore-
forming bacteria become the dominant community members. To ensure functional
redundancy, a multi-strain mixed inoculation strategy is adopted—urease-producing
bacteria can be simultaneously inoculated with Sporosarcina pasteurii, Bacillus
sphaericus, and Bacillus megaterium, such that if one strain declines, the others can
substitute for its function.

    Microbial community monitoring and early warning. It is recommended to establish
a microbial community monitoring and early warning system based on 16S rRNA high-
throughput sequencing: samples should be collected from different depths of the pile
body every 3 months to analyze changes in community composition and diversity. When
the relative abundance of functional bacterial groups declines by more than 50% from the
peak value, or when the Shannon diversity index decreases by more than 30% from the
baseline, an early warning should be triggered and supplementary inoculation procedures
initiated.


2.3.2 Black Soldier Fly Bioconversion System
    The black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens L.), belonging to the family Stratiomyidae of
the order Diptera, has emerged in recent years as the most widely studied saprophagous
insect species in the field of organic solid waste bioconversion. Its larvae feed on organic
waste and possess outstanding advantages including a broad dietary range, high
conversion efficiency, rapid growth rate, and non-vector status for disease transmission.

    Core conversion capacity. Under conditions of 25–30°C, black soldier fly larvae
require approximately 25 days from hatching to prepupal stage; one ton of larvae can
process 4 tons of livestock manure or 2.5 tons of food waste within a rearing cycle of
approximately 40 days. The larval body contains 35–45% crude protein (dry weight) and
25–35% crude fat (dry weight), while the frass is rich in humic acid and nitrogen,
phosphorus, and potassium. The recommended optimal inoculation density is 5,000–
10,000 individuals/m² (calculated based on the surface area of the organic mixture layer);
below the lower limit, conversion is insufficient, while above the upper limit,
intraspecific competition is triggered. In large-scale operations, a batch inoculation
strategy can be adopted: initial inoculation at 5,000 individuals/m², followed by
supplementation with 2,000–3,000 individuals/m² after 2 weeks depending on
consumption status.

    Synergistic bioconversion by black soldier fly and microorganisms. The larval gut
harbors a rich microbial community (Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, etc.),
which is excreted into the pile substrate during feeding, assisting in the decomposition of
recalcitrant organic matter such as cellulose, and secreting urease and carbonic anhydrase
to indirectly promote CaCO₃ precipitation. The feeding and burrowing activities of larvae
significantly alter the microbial community structure of the pile body: by ingesting
organic matter, they pulverize large material fragments into fine particles, substantially
increasing the surface area available to microorganisms (by 10–100 fold); the micro-
channels created within the pile body improve oxygen diffusion and gas exchange; and
the excreted frass, rich in readily degradable organic matter and active microorganisms,
forms distributed mineralization "hotspots" within the pile.

    Potential of other insect species. In addition to the black soldier fly, larvae of the
housefly (Musca domestica) can convert livestock manure within 4–5 days, with a protein
content as high as 50–60%; the yellow mealworm (Tenebrio molitor) can feed on
materials with higher cellulose content such as straw, and its gut microbiota possess
unique lignin-degrading capabilities; larvae of the white-spotted flower chafer (Protaetia
brevitarsis) can feed on mature compost and straw, producing high-quality frass rich in
organic matter and trace elements. In the future, suitable combinations of insect species
can be flexibly selected based on material characteristics and climatic conditions of
different regions.

    3 Technical Feasibility Assessment


3.1 Research Progress on MICP-Accelerated Steel Slag Carbonation

    A study by Professor Qian Chunxiang's team at Southeast University, published in
Engineering (the journal of the Chinese Academy of Engineering), combined microbial
technology with a rotary process, using carbonic anhydrase-producing microorganisms to
accelerate CO₂ fixation from cement kiln exhaust gas in steel slag powder, achieving a
CO₂ fixation rate more than double that of the control. Further research by the same team
employed suspended-state MICP technology, reducing the time required to achieve 10%
carbon sequestration to one-fifth of that needed by direct carbonation, and elucidated the
multiple acceleration mechanisms. When the carbon sequestration rate exceeds 8% and
the specific surface area is no less than 300 m²/kg, the volume stability issue of steel slag
can be effectively resolved (Qian et al., 2025).

    Regarding direct improvement of volume stability, Sporosarcina pasteurii has been
demonstrated to achieve synchronous CO₂ mineralization sequestration and f-CaO
removal under ambient conditions (30°C, atmospheric pressure). A multi-microorganism
(Sporosarcina pasteurii + yeast + carbonic anhydrase-producing bacteria) synergistic pre-
treatment method for steel slag has also been proposed. A green modification route
combining carbonic anhydrase-mediated microbial mineralization with boiling water pre-
treatment has been demonstrated at both macro- and micro-scales to effectively improve
the volume stability of steel slag fine aggregates.


3.2 Research on Synergistic Treatment of Steel Slag and Organic Waste

    Previous studies have successfully used steel slag as the primary cementitious
material, incorporating rice straw fibers to prepare foamed concrete, demonstrating the
feasibility of synergistic resource utilization of agricultural and industrial by-products. A
patented technology (CN120647217A) for preparing asphalt concrete using modified
steel slag combined with iron tailings and corn straw has confirmed the technical pathway
for synergistic utilization of effective components from multiple waste streams.

    In the field of environmental remediation, composite materials of steel slag and rice
straw biochar have exhibited excellent performance in the immobilization of heavy
metals (e.g., arsenic) in soil. Field trials have demonstrated that combined application of
steel slag and green waste compost significantly promotes the growth of durum wheat,
improves soil health, and enhances drought resistance (2025).
    At the industrial demonstration level, the CCUS technology industrial demonstration
project at HBIS Group Tangsteel New Area achieved, for the first time at industrial scale,
the integration of two technical routes: "blast furnace gas carbon capture coupled with
microbial carbon fixation for protein production" and "steel slag flue gas carbonation for
resource utilization," producing high-quality building materials with a carbon
sequestration capacity of 20% and compressive strength exceeding 30 MPa, which
received the "CarbXRock" trademark authorization (HBIS Group, 2026).


3.3 Large-Scale Black Soldier Fly Bioconversion Practice

    In China, the industry-scale treatment of organic waste using the black soldier fly has
become quite mature. The Yantian District of Shenzhen has established the nation's first
full-scale black soldier fly food waste treatment demonstration zone, cumulatively
processing over 55,000 tons of food waste, with an estimated carbon emission reduction
of 326 tons of CO₂ equivalent in 2025. The research team led by Professor Huang
Yongping at Shanghai Jiao Tong University has built a black soldier fly pilot line with a
daily processing capacity of 4 tons of food waste on campus. A black soldier fly project
in Fuyang, Hangzhou, with a daily processing capacity of 30 tons of organic waste, has
obtained commercial financial institution loan support. These cases fully demonstrate the
engineering feasibility and economic sustainability of black soldier fly technology in the
field of organic waste treatment.


3.4 Remanufacturing Pathways for Artificial Ore

    The end product of the AAD process—"artificial ore"—is dominated by CaCO₃ and
C-S-H gel as its primary mineral phases and is completely stabilized in nature. Its
utilization as building materials can follow four technical pathways depending on the
target product:

    Autoclaved products pathway. The artificial ore is crushed, mixed with appropriate
amounts of quicklime and gypsum in a specified ratio, and reacted in an autoclave
(approximately 180–200°C, approximately 1.0–1.5 MPa saturated steam pressure) for 6–
10 hours to produce autoclaved lime-sand bricks or aerated concrete blocks with
tobermorite (5CaO·6SiO₂·5H₂O) as the main hydration product, ensuring fully
guaranteed volume stability.

    Cement clinker pathway. The chemical composition of artificial ore (CaO-SiO₂-
Al₂O₃-Fe₂O₃) corresponds to a mixed mineral of "artificial limestone + clay." After
crushing and homogenization, it is fed into a rotary kiln and calcined at 1300–1450°C,
where the components recombine to form new cement clinker dominated by C₃S and C₂S.
This pathway is essentially a "secondary calcination and remanufacturing" of steel slag
after homogenization and stabilization in the AAD stage, thoroughly eliminating volume
stability risks at the raw material level.

    Carbonated building materials pathway. The artificial ore is crushed, pressed into
shape, and cured in a CO₂ atmosphere (concentration > 20%) for several hours to days.
Residual C-S-H gel reacts with CO₂ to form CaCO₃ and silica gel (SiO₂·nH₂O); the silica
gel fills pores while CaCO₃ serves as a cementitious phase, forming a dense calcium
carbonate-silica gel composite matrix. Steel mills' blast furnace gas (CO₂ content 20–25%)
or cement plant kiln exhaust gas (CO₂ content 15–30%) can be used as CO₂ sources. The
HBIS Group Tangsteel New Area CCUS project has already demonstrated the full
industrial feasibility of this route.

    Concrete aggregate pathway. After 5 years of mineralization stabilization, the f-CaO
content of artificial ore drops below 1%, and its volume stability fully meets the
requirements for concrete aggregates. Its apparent density is approximately 2.4–2.8 g/cm³,
water absorption rate approximately 3–6%, and crushing index approximately 10–15%,
meeting the Class II or Class III aggregate standards of GB/T 14685. Compared with
natural crushed stone, artificial ore aggregates possess unique advantages including a
rough porous surface, strong mechanical interlocking force with cement paste, a dense
interfacial transition zone, and residual CaCO₃ reactivity that can continuously participate
in carbonation-based micro-repair during the service life of concrete. With China's annual
concrete production exceeding 3 billion cubic meters and natural sand and gravel
aggregate resources becoming increasingly depleted, the potential market space for
artificial ore as a substitute aggregate is enormous.
    Artificial ore reactivity evaluation methods. A dedicated multi-index evaluation
system needs to be established: calcium carbonate content (TGA method, 600–850°C
weight loss attributed to CaCO₃ decomposition), residual f-CaO content (ethylene glycol-
ethanol method, GB/T 176), soluble CaO content (ammonium acetate solution extraction),
strength activity index (mixed with reference cement at a 3:7 ratio, 28-day compressive
strength ratio), etc.

    4 Preliminary Design of the Proof-of-Concept Engineering System


4.1 Site Selection and Scale

    Site Selection Criteria: The site shall be established on existing steel slag yards or
idle industrial lands associated with iron and steel enterprises, with priority given to the
utilization of abandoned industrial sites and degraded land. The site should be adjacent to
urban kitchen waste treatment plants and agricultural cultivation areas (to facilitate straw
collection and transport). The total land area is approximately 2–3 ha (30–45 mu).

    Treatment Capacity: Total processing volume is approximately 10,000 m³, of which
steel slag accounts for about 60% (6,000 m³, ~12,000 tons) and organic waste (straw,
kitchen waste, livestock manure, and biochar) accounts for approximately 40%. The
annual treatment capacity is approximately 2,400 tons of steel slag and 1,600 tons of
organic waste.
4.2 Heap Construction and Layer Sequence Design

    A "sandwich" layered sequence structure is adopted, arranged from bottom to top as
follows:

    (a) Foundation layer (30 cm): A composite anti-seepage system comprising
compacted clay + a double-layer HDPE geomembrane (2 mm) + a geosynthetic clay liner
(GCL). An electrically conductive leak detection grid is installed between the two
geomembrane layers; in the event of a leak, the resistance of the detection layer changes,
enabling immediate localization of the leak point and initiation of emergency response
measures.

    (b) Drainage layer (20 cm): Composed of gravel and a network of corrugated
perforated pipes, serving the dual functions of leachate collection and recirculation, as
well as gas (CO₂, CH₄) collection.

    (c) Main reaction layer (2–3 m): Materials are placed in alternating succession as
"steel slag layer (20 cm) → organic mixture layer (straw, kitchen waste, manure; 15 cm)
→ steel slag layer → biochar layer (5 cm)," forming 5–8 cyclic units. Water-quenched
converter steel slag is preferentially selected. The steel slag is classified by particle size:
coarse slag (20–40 mm) for the bottom layer, fine slag (5–15 mm) for the middle layer,
and powdered slag (<5 mm) for the top layer, thereby utilizing inter-particle pore size
differences to control reaction rate zonation. Black soldier fly larvae are inoculated into
the organic mixture layers (initial rate: 5,000 individuals/m², supplemented with 2,000–
3,000 individuals/m² after two weeks).

    After every 2–3 material layers are placed, moderate compaction is applied using a
light vibratory roller (static pressure 0.2–0.5 MPa) to simulate the overburden sediment
pressure effect, promoting tight inter-particle contact among steel slag particles while
avoiding excessive compaction that would impede gas diffusion.

    (d) Surface cover layer (20 cm): Composed of steel slag powder mixed with a small
amount of imported topsoil, amended with activated carbon or zeolite (5 wt%) to enhance
deodorization. Alkali-tolerant pioneer plant species such as Suaeda glauca and Cynodon
dactylon, as well as deodorizing plants such as mint (Mentha spp.) and lemongrass
(Cymbopogon citratus), are planted.

    (e) Methane oxidation cover layer (30–50 cm): A mixture of compost and wood
chips (1:1 by volume), which utilizes naturally enriched methanotrophic bacteria to
oxidize CH₄ into CO₂.

    (f) Leachate conditioning tank: Volume approximately 500–800 m³ (capable of
accommodating three months of leachate generation). The tank is divided into a normal
recirculation compartment and an overflow temporary storage compartment for the rainy
season. A floating cover is installed on top, and the tank is equipped with an online liquid
level meter and conductivity monitoring probe. When the conductivity exceeds 8 mS/cm,
the corresponding batch of leachate is diverted to an evaporation pond or mixed with
steel slag powder to prepare backfill material, and is no longer recirculated.

    (g) Black soldier fly breeding area: A mesh-enclosed facility (~10 m²) is established
adjacent to the heap. Prepupae are periodically collected from the heap and transferred to
the breeding area for eclosion, mating, and oviposition. The resulting egg masses are then
re-inoculated into the organic mixture layers of the heap.

    (h) Monitoring system: Temperature, moisture content, pH, and gas (O₂, CO₂, CH₄)
sensors equipped with data loggers are embedded at different depths within the heap (50
cm, 100 cm, 200 cm). At least three groundwater monitoring wells are installed around
the site (one upgradient, two downgradient), with quarterly sampling and analysis for pH,
heavy metals, COD, and other relevant parameters.


4.3 Operation and Regulation Scheme

    Months 0–3 (Rapid start-up phase): All layer sequences are placed in a single
construction campaign. Black soldier fly and MICP bacterial agents (Bacillus pasteurii,
Bacillus sphaericus, Bacillus megaterium, and CA-producing enzyme bacteria) are
inoculated. Leachate recirculation is initiated (twice per week, recirculation ratio 0.5:1).
Heap temperature (target: 55–65°C) and pH (target: 7–9) are monitored with priority.
    Months 4–12 (Main reaction phase): Black soldier flies undergo continuous
reproduction and bioconversion. Kitchen waste and straw are supplemented monthly.
Leachate recirculation frequency is reduced (once per week). Low-concentration CO₂
flue gas (5–10% CO₂, sourced from plant boiler exhaust) injection into the bottom of the
heap begins. Forced aeration is performed once per month (low-pressure blower, airflow
rate 0.5–1.0 L/min/kg) to maintain dissolved oxygen concentration within the heap above
2 mg/L. Natural plant colonization commences.

    Years 2–5 (Stabilized transformation phase): Supplementation of organic waste is
terminated. Leachate recirculation is reduced to once per month. Continuous CO₂ flue gas
injection is maintained. Heap sampling (core drilling) is conducted semi-annually for
analysis of f-CaO content, CaCO₃ content, mineral phase composition (XRD), micro-
morphology (SEM), and pore structure (mercury intrusion porosimetry, MIP). 16S rRNA
high-throughput sequencing of the microbial community is performed every three months.

    Odor control scheme: Biochar or zeolite (5–10% by volume) is incorporated into the
organic mixture layers to increase porosity and promote natural ventilation. Activated
carbon or zeolite (5 wt%) is added to the surface cover layer to capture H₂S and NH₃. A
biofilter (packed with wood chips, bark, and compost) is installed at the end of the gas
collection pipe network to oxidize H₂S, NH₃, and VOCs into SO₄²⁻ and NO₃⁻.

    Salt accumulation prevention and control: Approximately 10–15% of the total
leachate volume is discharged monthly, and the high-salinity leachate is directed to an
evaporation pond or mixed with steel slag powder to produce low-strength backfill
material. During the rainy season, fresh water is introduced through a rainwater collection
system for dilution. Halophytes (Suaeda glauca, Suaeda salsa) are planted on the heap
surface to absorb salt ions such as Na⁺ and K⁺.

    Methane control: When the methane concentration collected from the gas pipe
network exceeds 5% (v/v), a ground-level flare system is activated for combustion
treatment.

    End of Year 5 (Evaluation and mining phase): A comprehensive assessment of the f-
CaO conversion rate, mineral phase composition, and physical-mechanical properties of
each heap layer is conducted. The surface zone is retained as an ecological landscape.
The lower zones are selectively "mined," and the recovered "artificial ore" is sent to a
building materials remanufacturing pilot line for medium-scale testing of autoclaved
bricks, carbonated bricks, and aggregates.

    5 Strategic Value and National Significance


5.1 Establishing a National Strategic Reserve of Calcium-Silicon
Resources

    From a geological resource perspective, the 1.5 billion tons of steel slag historically
stockpiled in China, with a CaO content of approximately 40–50% and SiO₂ content of
approximately 10–15%, represents a highly substantial quantity of recoverable calcium-
silicon resources. Against the backdrop of limited natural limestone resources,
increasingly stringent mining restrictions, and the progressive depletion of high-quality
sand and gravel aggregates, these steel slag deposits constitute a veritable — and as yet
unrecognized — "artificial limestone ore vein." Through a controlled transformation
period of 5–10 years, AAD technology converts this "low-grade raw ore" into stable,
readily extractable "high-grade artificial ore." In essence, it transforms the current
environmental liability into a national strategic resource reserve capable of securing raw
material security for the building materials industry for decades to come.


5.2 Generating Massive Artificial Carbon Sinks

    The carbonation-based carbon sequestration potential of steel slag is theoretically
established: each ton of f-CaO that is fully carbonated can fix 0.78 tons of CO₂. Given
that silicate minerals such as C₂S and C₃S also exhibit carbonation reactivity, the
comprehensive carbon sequestration potential of steel slag is approximately 0.2–0.4 tCO₂
per ton of steel slag. If the existing 1.5 billion tons of historically stockpiled steel slag
were fully carbonated through the AAD system, 300–600 million tons of CO₂ could be
sequestered over the full life cycle. If the approximately 100 million tons of newly
generated steel slag each year were also incorporated into this system, an additional 20–
40 million tons of CO₂ could be fixed annually. The carbonation product, CaCO₃,
represents one of the most stable forms of carbon sequestration on a geological timescale,
far surpassing carbon stored in biomass form.

    Life-cycle carbon footprint analysis. Carbon sink side (positive benefits) includes:
steel slag carbonation, which fixes 0.2–0.4 tCO₂ per ton of steel slag; biochar stable
carbon sequestration, which fixes 0.05–0.1 tCO₂ per ton of organic waste; and plant
photosynthetic carbon fixation (during the ecological park stage), which sequesters 0.01–
0.02 tCO₂ per year per mu (approx. 0.067 ha). Carbon emission side (negative benefits)
includes: material transportation; leachate recirculation pumping (annual electricity
consumption of approximately 5,000–10,000 kWh); CO₂ injection fans; mechanical
compaction; and personnel operations and monitoring. Preliminary estimates indicate a
net carbon sink efficiency of approximately 70–80% of the theoretical carbon
sequestration capacity, corresponding to a net carbon sink of approximately 0.15–0.3
tCO₂ per ton of steel slag processed.

    Carbon sink classification and long-term monitoring. Carbon sinks should be
classified into three categories and measured separately: (1) Permanent carbon sink
(geological storage grade, half-life >10⁶ years): carbon fixed as CaCO₃ through steel slag
carbonation; (2) Long-term carbon sink (centennial scale, half-life approximately 100–
1,000 years): carbon fixed in biochar; (3) Temporary carbon sink (annual to decadal
scale): residual undecomposed organic carbon and plant biomass carbon remaining in the
stockpile. It is recommended to establish a multi-scale carbon sink monitoring system
comprising: stockpile-scale monitoring (CO₂ flux sensing probes); landfill-scale
monitoring (eddy covariance method for measuring net CO₂ flux); laboratory analysis
(TGA + EA for determining total carbon and inorganic carbon content); and isotope
tracing (¹³C-labeled CO₂ to track the pathways of carbon migration and transformation).

    Temporal value of carbon sinks. Steel slag carbonation achieves immediate carbon
sequestration — CO₂ is permanently fixed within hours to days, and the carbon sink
benefit is realized instantaneously. By contrast, afforestation-based carbon sinks require
10–20 years to reach maximum carbon sequestration rates and face reversal risks from
wildfires, pests, and diseases. Under the urgent timetable of the "dual carbon" goals
(carbon peak and carbon neutrality), technological solutions capable of generating
substantial, immediate carbon sinks in the near term possess irreplaceable strategic value.
The temporal advantage of steel slag carbonation as an instantaneous carbon sink
provides additional support for its pricing and competitiveness in carbon trading markets.

    Comparison with other carbon sink technologies. The unique advantages of steel slag
carbonation for carbon sequestration include: extremely high carbon stability (CaCO₃ is
one of the most stable carbon sink forms in the Earth's surface layer); synergy with solid
waste management (simultaneously achieving steel slag valorization and carbon
sequestration); and acceptable cost (leveraging natural forces and the leverage of time to
reduce energy consumption). In carbon trading markets, immediate carbon sinks typically
command a "temporal premium" — buyers are willing to pay higher prices for immediate
emission reductions.

    Carbon leakage prevention and control. Potential carbon leakage pathways include:
carbon emissions arising from land-use changes caused by land occupation for stockpile
sites; fuel-related carbon emissions from transporting organic waste; and reduced
alternative energy carbon savings when organic waste is diverted from its original
treatment pathway (e.g., incineration for power generation). Prevention and control
measures include: prioritizing the use of abandoned industrial land and already degraded
land for stockpile site construction; prioritizing organic waste sources within a 50 km
radius of the stockpile site; and prioritizing organic waste originally destined for landfill
disposal to avoid competition with high-energy-efficiency pathways such as incineration
for power generation.

    Carbon sink measurement and certification. The carbon sink volume from steel slag
carbonation has not yet been incorporated into existing carbon trading systems (e.g.,
CCER methodologies). It is recommended to collaborate with organizations such as the
China Quality Certification Center (CQC) to promote the development of a CCER
methodology for steel slag carbonation. Prior to approval of the methodology,
certification under international voluntary carbon reduction standards such as VCS
(Verified Carbon Standard) or GS (Gold Standard) may be pursued as an interim measure.


5.3 Catalyzing a New Industry: The "Artificial Ore Deposit Industry"

    The "Jianggang Plan" is not merely a technology; it has the potential to catalyze an
entirely new industrial category — the "artificial ore deposit industry." This industry
encompasses the entire chain: the refined geological design of stockpile sites (a form of
"reverse mining" that prepares for future extraction); long-term monitoring and process
regulation (analogous to mine pressure and hydrological monitoring); and future selective
extraction and building material remanufacturing. In terms of industrial form, it
represents a hybrid of the "storage + dispatch" model of strategic petroleum reserves and
the "ecology + landscape" model of national parks — a composite emerging industry that
integrates resource reserve management, carbon sink operations, ecological services, and
building materials production.


5.4 Deep Alignment with National Strategies

    AAD technology aligns closely with China's top-level national strategies across
multiple dimensions: it directly contributes to the carbon sink target of the "dual carbon"
goals; it perfectly fulfills the requirements of the "Ten Measures for Solid Waste"
concerning "strengthening control over historically stockpiled volumes" and "improving
the comprehensive utilization rate of bulk solid wastes"; it responds in a highly
innovative manner to the strategic deployment of "building a waste recycling system" in
the 15th Five-Year Plan for Circular Economy Development; and the ecological
succession outcome of "mining area transformed into park" provides a vivid practical
example from the field of industrial solid waste management that embodies the principle
that "lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets."

    6 Risks, Challenges, and Further Research Needs


6.1 Key Scientific Issues

    (a) Multi-factor synergistic kinetic modeling. The reactions occurring simultaneously
within the stockpile—including organic acid leaching, microbial mineralization, direct
carbonation, and hydration—exhibit complex coupling and competitive relationships.
Establishing a mathematical model that accurately describes the transformation kinetics
of f-CaO and the RO phase (MgO-FeO-MnO solid solution) under multi-factor synergy is
a prerequisite for process prediction and parameter optimization. Currently lacking are
experimental data on steel slag transformation kinetics under the ternary coupling
condition of "organic acid–MICP–CO₂."

    (b) Multi-field coupling of mass transfer, heat transfer, and reaction within the
stockpile. A 10,000-ton-scale stockpile constitutes a典型的 heterogeneous porous media
reactor, within which the distribution and evolution of the temperature field, moisture
field, gas concentration field, and chemical reactions are extremely complex. Multi-field
coupled numerical simulation methods tailored to steel slag–organic waste mixed
stockpiles need to be developed.
    (c) Long-term maintenance of functional microbial activity. Whether the inoculated
MICP functional bacterial consortium can maintain its activity over a 5-year timescale
within the continuously evolving chemical and physical environment of the stockpile is
the core microbiological issue determining system success. Long-term experiments are
needed to study the succession patterns and functional stability of the microbial
community. Bacteriophage infection is a significant risk factor that can lead to functional
microbial collapse; preventive and control measures include multi-strain rotational
inoculation, physical isolation via biochar micropores, environmental regulation
deviating from optimal phage reproduction conditions, and the establishment of a backup
strain bank.

    (d) Long-term stability of the RO phase. The RO phase (MgO-FeO-MnO solid
solution) undergoes slow hydration under natural conditions, which may still cause
volume changes over a timescale of decades. In the AAD environment, the organic acid
environment of Phase I can selectively react with MgO on the surface of the RO phase,
converting it into dissolved Mg²⁺, which is subsequently carbonated into stable MgCO₃.
The transformation effectiveness of the RO phase should be evaluated by monitoring the
amount of high-magnesium calcite formed.

    (e) Heavy metal migration, transformation, and risk control. Heavy metals in steel
slag (Cr, Ni, Pb, Zn, Cd, etc.) face the risk of activation and leaching under the low-pH
conditions (pH 4–6) of Phase I. However, the AAD system possesses a unique "self-
stabilization" mechanism: when the pH rises back to 8–10 in Phases II and III, dissolved
heavy metals can be re-immobilized through co-precipitation with CaCO₃, adsorption by
C-S-H gel, and complexation with oxygen-containing functional groups on biochar
surfaces. Recommendations: control organic acid production during the acidification
phase to avoid prolonged pH below 5; increase biochar dosage (from 5% to 8–10%);
conduct monthly sampling to monitor key heavy metal concentrations (Cr(VI), Ni, Pb,
etc.) in leachate; and establish an emergency leachate collection pond.

    (f) Steel slag–black soldier fly compatibility. The dose–response relationship
between steel slag incorporation ratio and black soldier fly growth and development must
be determined experimentally. Experimental design: set up steel slag incorporation
gradients (0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50% by mass) under laboratory conditions,
introduce 100 three-day-old larvae per group, observe continuously for 14 days, and
monitor larval survival rate, average body weight, pupation rate, adult emergence rate,
and heavy metal content in larvae and frass. Simultaneously, set up frass–steel slag
transformation experiments (frass:steel slag mass ratio gradient), cultivate under constant
temperature and humidity for 90 days, and monitor changes in f-CaO and CaCO₃ contents.
It is expected that the "slow-release" characteristics of humic acid and fulvic acid in the
frass will continue to exert a promoting effect during Phases II and III.


6.2 Key Engineering Challenges

    (a) Leachate management and recirculation optimization. The leachate generated by
the stockpile contains high concentrations of calcium ions, organic acids, and microbial
metabolites. The timing, flow rate, and water quality control of its recirculation require
fine-tuned regulation. When the COD in the leachate exceeds 5,000 mg/L or the NH₃-N
concentration exceeds 500 mg/L, a pretreatment pond should be introduced to utilize the
inherently high pH and high calcium characteristics of steel slag for chemical
precipitation pretreatment before recirculation.

    (b) Product homogeneity assurance. Due to spatial heterogeneity in temperature,
moisture, gas concentration, and microbial distribution within the stockpile, the mineral
composition and mechanical properties of the "artificial ore" may vary significantly
across different zones. Assurance measures include: rationally designing the spatial
distribution of leachate recirculation and CO₂ injection points; adopting zonal extraction
combined with mechanical stirring homogenization; and establishing a graded utilization
system—high-activity products (CaCO₃ > 60%, f-CaO < 1%) for cement clinker,
medium-activity products (CaCO₃ 30–60%) for autoclaved products, and low-activity
products (CaCO₃ < 30%) for carbonated building materials or engineering fillers.

    (c) Ecological risk prevention and control. The potential impact of the system on the
surrounding environment should be assessed from the following dimensions:
groundwater safety (upstream and downstream monitoring wells, quarterly testing for pH,
heavy metals, and COD, with early warning thresholds set); atmospheric environmental
impact (monitoring H₂S at the stockpile boundary not exceeding 0.06 mg/m³); soil and
biodiversity (semiannual heavy metal testing of soil within 50 m of the stockpile
perimeter, and surveys of plant and soil animal diversity).

    (d) Winter low-temperature countermeasures. In northern regions, the optimal
temperature for black soldier fly (25–30°C) is difficult to maintain naturally.
Countermeasure strategies: lay a 30–50 cm thick insulation layer of straw or wood chips
around the bottom and sides of the stockpile, utilizing internal bioheat to maintain core
temperature; breed low-temperature-adapted black soldier fly strains; and adopt a
rotational scheme of "black soldier fly as the primary transformation agent from April to
October + microorganism-dominated phase from November to March."

    7 Conclusions

    The "Jianggang Plan" proposed in this paper represents a fundamental leap in the

human engineering interventions within the grand material cycle and instead turns to

    The core academic contributions of this paper are as follows:

    For the first time, the theory of "Accelerated Artificial Diagenesis (AAD)" has been
systematically proposed, compressing geological-scale natural diagenesis into an
engineering-achievable 5-year cycle through multi-factor synergistic intensification;

    A multi-source waste synergistic共生体系 has been constructed, revealing five key
biochemical mechanisms: the coupling of organic acid release with calcium ion release,
the active utilization of steel slag self-pulverization, the coupling of bioheat generation
with self-heating maintenance, the coupling of in-situ CO₂ supply with carbonation, and
the coupling of straw skeleton structure with enhanced aeration;
    A "microorganism–insect–plant" tripartite synergistic transformation system has
been designed, achieving the organic integration of functional bacterial mineralization,
black soldier fly bioconversion, and pioneer plant ecological restoration;

    A five-year, three-phase ecological succession model has been proposed, establishing
a rigorous correspondence with geological diagenetic stages;

    A complete conceptual engineering design at the 10,000-ton scale has been
completed, demonstrating its scientific feasibility, engineering viability, and economic
rationality.

    This is not merely a technical solution, but a new resource perspective and
development philosophy. Its core insight is that the industrial waste currently regarded as
a burden, under appropriate spatiotemporal scales and ecosystem design, can fully be
transformed into a strategic resource for the future. This paradigm shift—from
"treatment" to "transformation" and from "burden" to "asset"—may well represent the
starting point for industrial civilization to move toward a "geological-scale circular
economy" in harmony with the Earth system.
